## Part 1: Worlds Inside Worlds

Look closely at a Russian nesting doll. Its painted wooden surface seems to tell you everything about it: a face, a scarf, a dress covered in flowers. But when you lift off the top, you discover a second doll inside. Open that one and you find a third, smaller still. The surprising part is not simply that the dolls become smaller. It is that each one has been made to fit inside another.

These dolls are called **matryoshka**. They make a simple idea visible: something that looks complete from the outside can be part of a much larger arrangement.

![An open red Russian nesting doll reveals a smaller painted doll inside, with three more dolls of decreasing size beside it.](/images/pensees/matryoshka/matryoshka-dolls.jpg)
*Russian nesting dolls, or **matryoshka**. Each doll opens to reveal a smaller one.*

I like to think about the universe in a similar way. When we examine something closely, we often discover that it belongs to a larger structure, or that it contains smaller structures we could not see at first.

The comparison isn't perfect. Galaxies and atoms are not hollow wooden containers. But the dolls give us a useful way to travel across distances that are otherwise almost impossible to imagine.

Let's begin far beyond Earth and work our way home.

### The largest view we can observe

On a clear night, you might see thousands of stars. That is an extraordinary sight, but it represents a tiny fraction of what exists.

The **universe** includes space, matter, and energy. We cannot yet see all of it, or know whether it has an overall boundary. The region we *can* observe, called the **observable universe**, is roughly **93 billion light-years across**. A light-year is not a measure of time; it is the distance light travels in one year. Space has expanded while that light has been traveling, which is one reason the observable universe can be so much wider than its age in years might suggest.

Within this enormous region are galaxies: vast collections of stars, gas, and dust. We will follow one particular path through them: toward the galaxy that contains our Sun.

[![A labeled diagram tracing the macro-scale journey from the observable universe to a galaxy, the solar system, and Earth.](/images/pensees/matryoshka/macro-scale-world.jpg)](/images/pensees/matryoshka/macro-scale-world.jpg){: .pensee-zoom}
*Our journey toward home: **Universe → Galaxy → Solar System → Earth**. Open the diagram for a larger view. It illustrates changing scales, not objects drawn at the same size.*

### Our place in the Milky Way

A **galaxy** is held together by gravity. Our galaxy, the **Milky Way**, contains more than 100 billion stars. Some are larger than our Sun, others smaller. Many have planets orbiting them.

From a dark place on Earth, the Milky Way appears as a faint band of light stretching across the sky. We see that band because we are looking through the crowded disk of our own galaxy. What looks like a cloud is actually the combined light of immense numbers of distant stars.

One of those stars, far from the galactic center, is our Sun. It is not unusual as stars go, but it matters enormously to us.

### Our Sun and its planets

The **Sun** is a star: a vast sphere of extremely hot matter, mostly hydrogen and helium. Its gravity holds eight planets in orbit, along with moons, asteroids, comets, and other smaller objects. Together, they form our **solar system**.

Sunlight needs about **eight minutes** to reach Earth. When you feel the Sun's warmth, the light carrying that energy left the Sun several minutes earlier. Even across the relatively modest distances of our solar system, light takes time to travel.

The third planet from the Sun is Earth. From space, it appears blue and white, marked by oceans, continents, and clouds. It is our next stop.

### Earth, a planet full of life

Earth rotates, giving us day and night, and completes an orbit around the Sun in about a year. As it moves along that orbit, it travels at roughly **30 kilometers per second**. We rarely think about this motion because everything around us is traveling with the planet.

More remarkable than Earth's movement is what has developed here: **life**. Oceans contain microscopic organisms and enormous whales. Forests support trees, insects, birds, and fungi. Human beings have built cities, written books, and invented machines that can explore the skies.

As far as we currently know, Earth is the only place where life has been confirmed.

From the scale of the observable universe, Earth is an almost unimaginably small destination. Yet we have only explored one direction: inward from space to a planet. There is another journey waiting at a much smaller scale, and its starting point is close enough to touch.

**Look at the skin on your fingertip. What is it actually made of?**

## Part 2: The World Beneath Your Fingertip

A fingertip seems smooth and solid, apart from the fine ridges that form your fingerprint. But its appearance depends on how closely you look.

Imagine using a microscope that could magnify the same tiny patch of skin again and again. First you would see its layers. Then individual cells. Beyond those cells are molecules and, at a still smaller scale, the atoms from which those molecules are built.

[![A labeled sequence showing the zoom from a finger to skin, a cell, DNA, and an atom.](/images/pensees/matryoshka/micro-scale-world.jpg)](/images/pensees/matryoshka/micro-scale-world.jpg){: .pensee-zoom}
*The journey beneath your skin: **Finger → Skin → Cell → DNA → Atom**. The highlighted areas indicate what we examine next. Open the diagram to see its details. It is a simplified illustration, not a set of objects shown at the same scale.*

### Skin is made of living cells

Skin is not a single sheet. It has several layers, each made of structures that perform different jobs. Among them are **cells**, the basic units of living things.

Some organisms, including many bacteria, consist of only one cell. Your body contains **tens of trillions**, with different kinds performing different tasks. Nerve cells carry signals. Muscle cells help you move. Skin cells help protect you from the outside world.

A typical living skin cell is itself an organized system. It has a membrane that separates its inside from its surroundings, structures that help keep it functioning, and a **nucleus** that holds much of its genetic information.

Inside that nucleus is a molecule with an especially important role: **DNA**.

### DNA: information written in chemistry

**DNA** carries genetic instructions that cells use to develop and function. It is arranged in long strands, built from repeating units that come in four chemical varieties. The sequence of those units stores information, somewhat as different arrangements of letters can produce different words.

If you could stretch out the DNA from the nucleus of one typical human cell, it would measure roughly **two meters**. Yet it fits into a nucleus that is only a few micrometers across, because it is folded and packed into an extremely compact form.

This raises an interesting question. DNA is incredibly small to us, but it is not the smallest thing in this journey. What is the molecule itself made of?

### Atoms, and the particles inside them

DNA, like water, sugar, and the rest of your body, is built from **atoms**. Different kinds of atoms join in different combinations to form molecules.

An atom has a tiny central **nucleus**, containing protons and, in most atoms, neutrons. Around it is a region where electrons are found. School diagrams often show electrons following circular paths, like planets orbiting a star. That is a helpful symbol for recognizing an atom, but it is not how electrons actually behave. Physics describes them using regions of probability, sometimes called **electron clouds**.

Protons and neutrons contain even smaller particles known as **quarks**. Scientists have investigated whether quarks themselves have smaller components, but no such internal structure has been observed.

Here the matryoshka analogy reaches one of its limits. A cell contains DNA, and DNA is *made from* atoms, but those are different relationships. Nature is not always a sequence of little containers. Our illustrations trace **levels of structure and scale**, rather than literal dolls sitting inside one another.

Let's see the entire journey together. The nesting dolls bring the large and small worlds into one picture, from the universe and its galaxies to living organisms, cells, molecules, and atoms.

![Nested colored ovals, each inside the next, with a Russian nesting doll under each label, growing in size. From smallest to largest: atom, molecule, cell, organism, planet, solar system, galaxy, universe.](/images/pensees/matryoshka/matryoshka-universe.jpg)
*The Matryoshka Universe: eight levels of organization, connected by the nesting-doll analogy. Some levels are containers; others are structures made from smaller components.*

Still, this journey has revealed something remarkable. The atoms in your fingertip are far older than your cells. Most of them are even older than Earth itself.

To understand why, we need to stop zooming inward and begin traveling backward through time.

## Part 3: Where Our Atoms Came From

Everything we've explored, from galaxies to living cells, depends on matter. But matter has a history. The atoms in your body were not created when you were born, and most were not created when Earth formed.

Their story begins long before our solar system existed.

### Before the first stars

About **13.8 billion years ago**, the early universe was extraordinarily hot and dense. As it expanded and cooled, the first simple atomic nuclei formed. Later, electrons combined with nuclei to make atoms. Most of the ordinary matter at that stage consisted of **hydrogen and helium**, the lightest elements.

At first there were no stars or planets. Over time, gravity drew enormous regions of gas together. As those clouds collapsed, the centers became hot and dense enough for stars to ignite.

The universe now had brilliant sources of light, and places where new kinds of atoms could be produced.

### The elements made in stars

Inside stars, nuclear reactions can combine light nuclei into heavier ones. Stars helped produce elements such as **carbon and oxygen**, which are essential to living organisms on Earth.

When certain stars reached the ends of their lives, they released material back into space. Exploding stars and collisions involving stellar remnants also helped produce and spread heavier elements. Over billions of years, these processes enriched the gas and dust from which later stars and planets would form.

The carbon in your cells and the oxygen you breathe were made inside ancient stars. Almost all the hydrogen in your body is older still, dating back to the early universe.

In that sense, the materials that make up your body have traveled through an astonishing history before becoming part of you.

### From ancient matter to a living planet

Around **4.6 billion years ago**, a cloud of gas and dust began to collapse in a region of the Milky Way. Most of the material gathered into the Sun. Some of what remained formed planets, including Earth.

Our planet was once far hotter and very different from the world we know today. Its surface changed, oceans eventually formed, and life appeared. Scientists have strong evidence that life existed billions of years ago, but **the exact steps by which the first living systems arose remain unresolved**.

Over vast stretches of time, life diversified. The history of Earth eventually included fish, forests, dinosaurs, birds, mammals, and human beings.

So the relationship between the universe and your body is deeper than a resemblance to nesting dolls. You are made of matter whose origins reach back to the young universe and to stars that lived long before the Sun.

The universe did not merely provide a place for life to exist. Its history made the ingredients of life possible.

## The Doll That Knows

In December 1968, the astronauts aboard Apollo 8 became the first humans to orbit the Moon. During that mission, Bill Anders photographed Earth rising above the lunar horizon. The image, known as [*Earthrise*](https://science.nasa.gov/resource/apollo-8s-iconic-earthrise/), showed our planet suspended against the darkness of space.

For most of human history, people had studied the sky from Earth. Now they could look back at Earth from far away. The photograph changed the point of view, and with it, perhaps, the way many people thought about our place in the universe.

We have learned to do something similar at both ends of the scale. **Telescopes** reveal distant planets and galaxies. **Microscopes** uncover cells and organisms invisible to our eyes. Particle detectors help scientists investigate the structure of matter.

Yet every discovery raises more questions. What is dark matter? How did life begin? Are there living worlds beyond Earth? Is there more to the structure of matter than we currently understand?

![Three children exploring the universe together: one looks through a microscope, one studies the sky with a telescope, and one examines Russian nesting dolls.](/images/pensees/matryoshka/young-explorers.jpg)
*Three ways to begin investigating: examine what is nearby, explore what is far away, and ask how different things fit together.*

You don't have to be an astronaut or a scientist to start asking these questions. You can observe the Moon over several nights and notice how its shape changes. You can examine a leaf with a magnifying glass, build a simple telescope, or draw the structures you have learned about and test whether you can explain how they connect.

We began with a wooden doll that could be opened to reveal another. In nature, each closer look can reveal an unexpected kind of complexity. And unlike the wooden dolls, we have no reason to assume that every remaining mystery has already been solved.

There is one last thought I find especially striking. The atoms that make up your brain obey the same laws of nature as the atoms in distant stars. Yet together they allow you to think, to question, and to investigate the world around you.

**You are part of the universe, and you can study the universe of which you are a part.**

Perhaps the next discovery will begin with a question that occurs to you.

### Words to know

- **Matryoshka:** A Russian wooden nesting doll that opens to reveal smaller dolls.
- **Galaxy:** A vast system of stars, gas, and dust held together by gravity.
- **Solar system:** The Sun and the planets and other objects that orbit it.
- **Cell:** The basic structural unit of living organisms.
- **Molecule:** A group of atoms joined together, such as water or DNA.
- **DNA:** A molecule that carries genetic instructions.
- **Atom:** A basic unit of a chemical element, made of a nucleus and electrons.
- **Element:** A substance made from one kind of atom, such as hydrogen, carbon, or oxygen.

### Try this: Draw two journeys through the universe

You need paper, pencils, and your imagination. Work with an adult if you use any scientific equipment.

1. Draw four connected panels for the **observable universe, the Milky Way, the solar system, and Earth**. In each panel, mark the specific place you will examine in the next one.
2. Draw a second sequence showing **a fingertip, skin, a cell, DNA, and an atom**. Use small boxes or circles to show what you're magnifying, just as a scientist might select a region of interest in an image.
3. Choose one element present in your body, perhaps **hydrogen, carbon, or oxygen**. Find out whether it was produced mainly in the early universe or in stars.
4. Explain your drawings to someone else. Which stages represent something *contained within* something larger? Which represent things *made from* smaller components?

That last distinction matters. Good scientific comparisons make complicated ideas easier to understand, but good scientists also know when a comparison stops being accurate.
